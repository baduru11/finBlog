On May 13 the Treasury sold \$25 billion of 30-year bonds at a high yield of [5.046%](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0489), the first 30-year auction to clear above 5% since August 2007. The issue tailed the when-issued level by 0.5 bps and printed a bid-to-cover of 2.303 — the weakest reading since November 2025. Nine days later, on May 22, Justice Clarence Thomas swore in Kevin Warsh as Federal Reserve Chair in the East Room of the White House, with President Trump in attendance. The last Fed chair to take the oath at the White House was Alan Greenspan, in 1987.

Wire copy has stitched these two facts into one story: hot April CPI plus a hawkish new chair equals "next Fed move is a hike," which is why the long end is selling off. [Fed-funds futures now imply a roughly 51% probability of a December 2026 hike](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/15/traders-now-see-next-fed-interest-rate-move-as-a-hike-following-inflation-surge.html), up from near zero a month ago.

The curve says something different.

## What the consensus is pricing

The hot-CPI-into-Warsh-hike story is at least internally coherent. April [CPI](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.htm) printed +0.6% month-over-month and 3.8% year-over-year, the highest YoY since May 2023. Core CPI was +0.4% MoM, 2.8% YoY. Supercore — services ex-shelter, ex-energy — climbed to 3.5% YoY from 3.4% in March. <ConceptBox term="Supercore CPI">Services excluding shelter and energy. Treated by the FOMC as the cleanest read on demand-side inflation, since it strips out volatile commodity and lagged-housing components.</ConceptBox>

The [Atlanta Fed's GDPNow](https://www.atlantafed.org/cqer/research/gdpnow) is tracking 4% real growth for Q2 2026, with unemployment at 4.3%. That is not an economy that needs cuts. Warsh's own confirmation-hearing language — that the Fed needs "a different framework" for assessing inflation — reads hawkish against Powell's late-2025 willingness to look through energy.

A bond market that processes all of that and reprices 30-year yields up roughly 60 bps in a month is doing the obvious thing.

## Except it isn't

A policy-rate repricing leads at the front end. The 2-year prices the discounted path of fed funds over the next eight FOMC meetings. If the market actually expected Warsh to hike in December, the 2-year would move first, and the curve would bear-flatten — short rates rising more than long rates.

That is not the trade on the screen.

- The 2-year closed at 4.13% on May 22. That is roughly 5 bps **below** its May 1 close of 4.18%.
- The 10-year closed at 4.56%, up from 4.39% on May 1, a 17 bps rise.
- The 30-year sits near 5.10%, having printed as high as 5.13% mid-week.
- The 2s10s spread is +43 bps, against +21 bps at the end of April — the curve has doubled in slope in three weeks, with the 2-year actually falling.

That is the signature of a bear steepener driven entirely by the long end. <ConceptBox term="Bear steepener">A curve move in which long rates rise faster than short rates. Typically driven by supply, term-premium, or long-horizon inflation-risk repricing — not by a near-term policy-rate repricing, which would flatten the curve.</ConceptBox> What is selling off here is duration risk, not the rate path.

The [NY Fed ACM model](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/data_indicators/term-premia) puts the 10-year term premium at 0.81% as of May 15, up from 0.68% on April 24 and the highest reading since 2023. The 13-bp rise in the term-premium estimate accounts for the bulk of the 17-bp move in the 10-year yield itself; the rest is a small revision to expected policy rates. At the 30-year, the term-premium share of the move is larger still.

<Callout variant="insight" label="Takeaway">
The 30-year above 5% is not the bond market betting on Warsh hikes. It is the bond market re-pricing the term premium it demands for holding US duration in a regime where fiscal trajectory, energy shocks, and central-bank independence are simultaneously in question. The May 22 White House oath was a term-premium event, not a policy-path event.
</Callout>

## Why the distinction matters

A bear-flattener and a bear-steepener look identical in mark-to-market P&L on a long-duration book. They differ entirely in what unwinds them.

If the consensus story were right — Warsh hikes in December, market repriced — the unwind comes from a Fed pivot or a soft data print. A weak May employment report would flatten the curve from the front end, and the 30-year would rally as terminal-rate expectations drop. The trade is short 2s, long 30s.

If the term-premium story is right, the unwind requires the opposite: a credible signal that the Fed will defend its mandate without political interference, that the borrowing path can be contained without forcing coupon increases, and that the energy shock is genuinely transitory. Soft data alone does not pull a positive term premium back toward zero — it can widen it, if soft data raises deficit anxiety. The trade is long 2s, short 30s; a curve bear-steepener gets worse before it gets better.

The first real test is the June 16–17 FOMC, Warsh's debut meeting. A hawkish hold with "data-dependent" language restored, no SEP shift toward cuts, and an explicit upward revision to the long-run neutral rate would do more for the 30-year than a single hike. A hike with dovish forward guidance lifts the front end and compresses term premium — and the 30-year actually rallies. The trade book that says "short duration because Warsh will hike" is the inverse of the trade that has actually been printing in May.

## What I'm watching

- **May 28 Q1 GDP second estimate.** Watch the consumption revision. If final domestic demand revises higher, the 3.5% supercore reading gets harder to dismiss as energy passthrough.
- **June 16–17 FOMC, Warsh debut.** Three signals in order: (1) is "data-dependent" back in the statement, (2) does the SEP long-run dot move above 3.0%, (3) does Warsh's presser repeatedly cite "Fed independence" — a tell he wants term premium back out of the curve.
- **June 12 30-year reopening.** A second consecutive tailing 30-year, with bid-to-cover below 2.30, would mark the May 13 print as structural, not idiosyncratic.
- **ACM 10-year term premium, weekly.** A sustained move above 1.00% would put the estimate at its highest since 2014 and pressure Treasury to address coupon sizing into the August QRA.
- **Fed-funds futures December hike pricing.** A drop below 30% with the 30-year still above 5% would be the cleanest confirmation that the long end is doing something other than tracking the policy path.
