Two BLS releases landed in 24 hours, and they pointed in the same direction more cleanly than the wire copy made it sound. [April CPI](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.htm), out May 12, printed +0.6% month-over-month and 3.8% year-over-year — the highest YoY since May 2023. [April PPI](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ppi.nr0.htm), out May 13, came in at +1.4% MoM and +6.0% YoY, the largest annual jump since December 2022. The 30-year Treasury auction the same afternoon cleared at 5.046%, the first 30-year to print with a 5% handle since August 2007.

The headline reading is "tariffs are showing up." The data underneath says: yes, but not where 2018 trained the market to look.

## Did tariffs cause this?

If the April pass-through were running through goods at the till, you would see it most cleanly in the tariff-watchlist categories. They are the items where the duty schedule lands hardest and the supply chain is least domestic. The April reading on those lines:

- Apparel: +0.6% MoM, decelerating from +1.0% in March and +1.3% in February.
- Household furnishings and supplies: **-0.5%** MoM.
- New vehicles: **-0.2%** MoM.
- Used cars and trucks: flat.
- Recreation commodities: +0.1% MoM.
- Goods less food and energy commodities, aggregate: **0.0%** MoM.

That is not a goods-led inflation print. The categories you would expect to break higher if tariff costs were passing through to shelf prices either decelerated or declined. The 2018–19 cycle showed up first in washing machines and appliances; the 2026 cycle, four months into a much larger duty schedule, is not showing up there at all.

What broke higher was services — and the services line in the PPI is where the actual pass-through is happening.

## The evidence is in the wholesale data

The April PPI for final demand services rose 1.2% on the month, the largest increase since March 2022. Two-thirds of that move came from a 2.7% MoM jump in margins for final demand trade services. <ConceptBox term="PPI trade services margins">The PPI captures the difference between what wholesalers and retailers pay for goods and what they sell them for. A jump in this line is, by definition, a margin expansion — distributors capturing more of the spread, not retailers passing duty costs through to consumers.</ConceptBox>

That is the tell. Importers and wholesalers facing higher landed costs from April-tranche tariffs are not raising shelf prices on the affected SKUs — they are widening the margin between purchase and resale, particularly in trade and distribution channels. The duty is being absorbed at point-of-sale and recovered upstream through services pricing and through higher fees on services adjacent to the good (delivery, installation, financing, extended warranties).

CPI services confirms it. <ConceptBox term="Supercore CPI">Services excluding shelter and energy — the FOMC's preferred read on sticky, demand-side inflation.</ConceptBox> Supercore climbed to 3.5% YoY (from 3.4% in March) and +0.5% MoM. Airfares were a standout, partly an Iran-shock pass-through into jet fuel and ticket pricing, but the breadth across services categories — personal care, recreation services, transportation services — is too wide to be one Middle East story.

## Why the channel matters

A goods-led tariff pass-through is mechanically slow and has a known endpoint: once inventories turn over and the duty fully prices in, headline normalises. The Fed can model it, and the supercore measure was specifically designed to look past it.

A services-and-margin-led pass-through is different in three ways.

First, it is **stickier**. Services prices reprice with a lag and reset upward at contract renewals (rents, insurance, professional services). Once raised, they do not unwind even if the duty schedule is pared back.

Second, it is **harder to attribute**. Wholesale margin expansion looks indistinguishable from "pricing power" — Fed staff and sell-side desks reading the PPI line in isolation may classify it as margin recovery from Q4's compression rather than tariff recovery from inventory turn. That risks under-counting the tariff cost when it is being explicitly absorbed and re-priced one layer up.

Third, it is **the wrong inflation for the Fed's framework**. The FOMC has spent two years building communication around "transitory goods, sticky services." A pass-through that arrives as services inflation gets treated as evidence of demand, not as evidence of tariff cost. That asymmetry biases policy toward holding rates higher.

<Callout variant="insight" label="Takeaway">
The April CPI surprise was not tariffs failing to pass through. It was tariffs passing through the wrong line item. A retailer that absorbs a 12% duty on apparel and recovers it as a 4% services-margin expansion and a 6% increase in financing fees has, in BLS terms, produced no apparel inflation and material services inflation. That looks like demand to the FOMC. It is supply.
</Callout>

## What to watch

- **May 28 PCE.** The Fed's preferred measure. If services PCE prints similarly to services CPI — above +0.4% MoM — the June SEP will have to acknowledge a services-side problem the March SEP did not see.
- **ISM Services prices paid (May, June).** April held at 70.7. Sustained above 70 would confirm wholesale-margin recovery is broad, not one-month noise.
- **Retailer Q1 earnings reports through June.** Watch gross-margin commentary against the PPI trade-services line. If retailers post in-line margins while wholesalers post margin expansion, the absorption-then-recovery channel is confirmed.
- **NFIB small-business pricing-intentions, May release.** A jump in firms planning to raise prices in the next three months — particularly in services categories — is the leading indicator for whether the channel is broadening.
- **June 16–17 FOMC SEP.** A move in the long-run unemployment rate higher or in the long-run neutral rate higher would signal the Fed has read the supercore reacceleration as structural, not transitory.
