The Federal Reserve held the policy rate at 3.50–3.75% on March 18 — exactly as priced in. The press conference was dovish-ish but not surprising. The actual news was buried in the [Summary of Economic Projections](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/files/fomcprojtabl20260318.pdf): the committee disagrees with itself more than it has in years, and it raised its long-run policy rate to 3.0%.

That's a much bigger signal than the rate level.

## What the dots actually said

The headline: median 2026 path implies one 25-bp cut by year-end. But the median is hiding a striking spread. Among the 19 participants:

- 7 see no cuts in 2026
- 7 see one 25-bp cut
- 2 see 50 bps of cuts
- 2 see 75 bps
- 1 sees a full 100 bps

That's a 100-bp range across the committee, with the median basically a coin flip between zero cuts and one cut. For comparison, the December 2025 SEP had a much tighter cluster: a clear majority within a 25-bp band.

<ConceptBox term="What dot dispersion is telling you">
A widening dispersion is rarely about the dots themselves. It's about what's
happening to the data underneath. When inflation is clearly trending one way,
the committee converges. When the data is genuinely ambiguous — supply shocks,
crosscurrents in services vs goods, fiscal noise — participants disagree, and
the dots fan out. Read dispersion as a measure of *how confused the data is*,
not how confused the committee is.
</ConceptBox>

## The long-run rate is the bigger move

Easier to miss but more consequential: the longer-run dot — the committee's collective view of "neutral" — moved up to 3.0% from 2.875% in December. That doesn't sound like much, but the long-run rate had been anchored at 2.5% as recently as 2024. It has now climbed five times in successive SEPs.

That tells you the committee is quietly ratifying a structural shift: rates are not going back to where they were. The post-2022 regime is the new normal.

If neutral is 3.0% and the policy rate sits at 3.625% (midpoint of the current band), the Fed is barely restrictive. That's a very different framing than "we're tight and will need to cut" — and it's the framing that explains why the median dot is one cut, not three. From the committee's perspective, you don't need to ease much when you're not very tight to begin with.

## Why the bond market liked this less than equities

The 10-year Treasury closed March 18 at 4.34%, basically unchanged on the decision but up 4 bps on the day after the press conference. That's a small bear-steepener — the front end fell, the long end rose. The market is reading "no cuts and a higher long-run rate" as a higher [term premium](/concepts#term-premium) story, not a Fed-credibility story.

Equities reacted differently: S&P 500 +0.5% post-decision, recovering most of the morning's losses. The equity read is simpler — "no surprise, growth still positive, earnings season ahead." Different markets tell different stories from the same data. That's normal.

## What you should do with this

Three useful framings, in order of how confident I am in them:

1. **Don't fight the long-run dot.** When the committee shifts neutral, the entire term structure has to follow eventually. Rates products with optionality — like cap-floor structures that pay if rates stay above 3.0% — got more interesting on March 18.
2. **Watch dispersion, not the median.** The next two CPI prints (March 18-released for February tomorrow, April 10 for March) will compress or widen the dispersion. That movement matters more than which way the median goes.
3. **The pause is not a hold-then-cut.** The market is pricing one cut by December 2026; the dots agree. The dots don't agree on anything beyond that. Don't let the median fool you into a false-precision view of where rates land.

## What I'm watching next

- **April 10 CPI** for March data. If supercore prints below 0.30% MoM, the dovish dots have a story. If it prints above 0.40%, the hawkish dots get vocal.
- **The April SLOOS** ([Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey](https://www.federalreserve.gov/data/sloos.htm)). If banks tighten further, the Fed's "barely restrictive" framing gets harder to sustain.
- **5y5y forward inflation breakevens.** Currently 2.24%. If they break above 2.50% on the Iran story, the dovish dots fold quickly.
- **The May 7 FOMC.** The next meeting is six weeks out, but [Fed-speak](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/calendar.htm) between now and then is going to do a lot of work on shaping where the median goes in June.

What the dot plot really showed on March 18 wasn't a forecast. It was a confidence interval. And it's wider than it's been in a long time.
