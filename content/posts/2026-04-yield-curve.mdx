For most of 2024 and 2025, the US Treasury yield curve was inverted — the 2-year yield was higher than the 10-year. By textbook, an inverted curve has preceded every US recession since 1969. By the same textbook, the curve *un-inverting* — steepening back to a normal upward slope — is supposed to be the recession signal that actually fires.

So what's happening now, and how worried should you be?

## What "the curve" actually is

<ConceptBox term="Yield curve">
  A line plotting the yields of US Treasuries across maturities — typically
  3-month, 2-year, 5-year, 10-year, and 30-year. In normal times it slopes
  upward (longer = riskier = higher yield). When it slopes down, that's
  &ldquo;inversion.&rdquo;
</ConceptBox>

The 2s10s curve — the spread between the 2-year and 10-year yield — is the most-watched single number in fixed income. Today it sits at +18 basis points, having moved from -41 bps in November.

That's a 59-bp swing in five months. Real, but not dramatic by historical standards.

## Two ways a curve can steepen

Here's where most headlines get sloppy. There are two very different kinds of steepening, and they say opposite things about the economy:

1. **Bull steepener.** Both yields fall, but short rates fall *more*. The market is pricing in Fed cuts. This is what happens when the economy is slowing and the Fed is expected to ease.
2. **Bear steepener.** Both yields rise, but long rates rise *more*. The market is pricing in higher inflation expectations or a higher [term premium](/concepts#term-premium). This is what happens when the economy is running hot or fiscal supply is overwhelming demand for duration.

Today's move is mostly a bull steepener — the 2-year has fallen 38 bps since November while the 10-year is down 9 bps. That's the "Fed is going to cut" trade, not the "duration is getting punished" trade.

## What the term premium is doing

The other half of the story is the [term premium](/concepts#term-premium) — the extra yield investors demand for holding longer-dated bonds, beyond what's implied by expected future short rates. It collapsed to roughly zero in 2020 and has been climbing since.

The Adrian-Crump-Moench (ACM) model, which the New York Fed publishes weekly, currently estimates the 10-year term premium at +42 bps — the highest since 2014. That alone explains why the long end isn't falling as fast as the short end: investors *want* compensation for holding long duration in a world where Treasury issuance is heavy and inflation, while declining, is still uncomfortably above target.

<ConceptBox term="Why the term premium can't be observed directly">
  Future short rates aren't known, so the term premium has to be *estimated*.
  Different models (ACM, Kim-Wright, etc.) can give answers that disagree by
  20 bps or more. Treat any single point estimate as a guide, not a fact.
</ConceptBox>

## What the steepening is actually telling you

If you take the bull steepener at face value: the market expects the Fed to cut roughly three times by end-2026. If you take the term premium at face value: the long end isn't a bargain — investors are getting paid more than usual to hold duration because conditions warrant it.

Combine them, and the message is: *not a recession yet, but the conditions for one are being priced in*. That's exactly what the curve is supposed to do.

## What I'm watching next

- **Fed cuts vs. priced cuts.** If the Fed cuts faster than the market expects, the bull steepener accelerates and you should expect equities to flinch. The May 7 FOMC will tell us a lot.
- **The 30-year auction tail.** When 30-year auctions tail by more than 2 bps, it's a sign that the long end is struggling for buyers. Two consecutive tails — the next one is on May 14 — would tell me the term premium is repricing higher again.
- **The 3m10y curve.** Less famous than 2s10s, but the version the [New York Fed](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/capital_markets/ycfaq) itself uses for its recession-probability model. It's still inverted by 8 bps as of April 22, 2026.

Daily Treasury yield data on which all of this rests comes from the [Treasury's daily par yield curve rates](https://home.treasury.gov/policy-issues/financing-the-government/interest-rate-statistics) — worth bookmarking if you want to follow along.

The shape of the curve is one of the cleanest signals finance has. It just doesn't always say what people think it says.
