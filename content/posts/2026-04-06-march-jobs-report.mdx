The March [Employment Situation](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.nr0.htm) was a strange report. Nonfarm payrolls printed +178,000 for the month — three times the Dow Jones consensus of 59,000 and a sharp reversal of the -133,000 February print. The unemployment rate, somehow, also fell, from 4.4% to 4.3%. Both numbers were "good." Underneath, they tell different stories.

## The headline beat was real, but narrow

Of the 178,000 jobs added:

- Health care: +76,000 (43% of the total)
- Construction: +21,000
- Transportation and warehousing: +18,000
- Federal government: -14,000

That's a heavily concentrated print. Health care has been the single biggest source of payroll growth for nearly two years; March extended the streak. Strip it out and the rest of the economy added 102,000 jobs — solid but not the blowout the headline suggested. Wage growth was 0.2% month-on-month, 3.5% year-on-year — well below the 4%+ pace of mid-2024.

So: the labor market isn't weakening, but the *quality* of growth is narrowing. That's a familiar late-cycle pattern.

## The unemployment-rate beat was less real

This is the part that gets glossed in the wire copy. The unemployment rate didn't fall because hiring outpaced workforce growth. It fell because the *labor force shrank*. The labor force participation rate dropped to 62.1%, its lowest since early 2022. About 350,000 people left the workforce in March — more than the headline payroll gain.

<ConceptBox term="Why the unemployment rate can fall for the wrong reasons">
The unemployment rate is calculated as unemployed / (employed + unemployed).
If unemployed people stop looking — get discouraged, retire, or simply drop
out — they are no longer counted as unemployed. The rate falls without anyone
finding a job. This is exactly why the BLS publishes alternative measures
like U-6 (which includes discouraged and underemployed workers); U-6 actually
*rose* in March, from 7.9% to 8.0%.
</ConceptBox>

A falling participation rate alongside a falling unemployment rate is rarely good news. It is the labor-market equivalent of the bond market's "good for the wrong reason."

## What the Fed sees

The committee has been clear that they are watching three things: services-ex-housing inflation (the supercore), wages, and unemployment. On March data they got: 3.5% wage growth (cooling), 4.3% unemployment (stable on the headline, weaker underneath), and a CPI print due April 10 that will be heavily Iran-distorted.

That is not a labor market that demands a cut. It also is not a labor market that bars one. Which means the May 7 FOMC meeting is going to be entirely a story about what oil and CPI do in the next month, not about labor.

The market, predictably, did not move much on the print. The 2-year Treasury yield sits at 4.18%, basically flat on the day. Fed funds futures still imply roughly 30% probability of a cut on May 7 and 65% by July. Those are essentially unchanged from before the print.

## The thing nobody noted

One overlooked detail in the March report: federal government payrolls fell by 14,000, the seventh consecutive month of decline. The cumulative drag from federal layoffs since September 2025 is now roughly 110,000 jobs. That's a small number against total employment of 159 million, but it is concentrated in DC, Virginia, and Maryland — and shows up in those regional labor markets as visibly weak prints. If you live somewhere with high federal exposure, the macro picture is materially worse than the national headline.

This is a useful reminder that "the economy" is always a weighted average of regional economies, and the weights change.

## What I'm watching next

- **April CPI on May 12.** [BLS calendar](https://www.bls.gov/schedule/news_release/cpi.htm). The print will tell us how much Iran's energy spike fed into core. The market is pricing core +0.32% MoM; below 0.30% reopens the cuts conversation.
- **April jobs report on May 2.** Look at the participation rate first, the headline second. If 62.1% holds, the falling unemployment rate is a fiction. If it climbs back to 62.4%+ and unemployment stays at 4.3%, the labor market really is tightening.
- **Quits rate from JOLTS** (next print mid-April). It's been falling steadily for 18 months — currently 1.9%, down from a 2022 peak of 3.0%. A continued decline says workers are not confident enough to leave jobs voluntarily; that's the strongest leading indicator of softer wage growth.
- **The May 7 FOMC.** Will not cut. The interesting question is what Powell says about the dispersion in the [March SEP](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/files/fomcprojtabl20260318.pdf) — whether the committee is trending toward consensus or away.

The headline this morning will be "strong jobs report." It was not. It was a complicated jobs report whose median number happened to print high. Read the second decimal place; it is where the truth is.
