Two weeks into the conflict, Brent sits at $87 a barrel, up about 24% from the late-February pre-shock price near $70. The S&P 500 is down 3.4% over the same window. The 10-year Treasury yield is up 8 bps to 4.42%. Those are the numbers everyone is watching.

The number nobody is watching is the one that controls all of them: tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz.

## The chokepoint nobody can route around

The Strait connects the Persian Gulf to the open ocean. It is 21 miles wide at its narrowest point, with an inbound and outbound shipping lane each two miles wide. Roughly 20–21 million barrels per day of crude and condensate transit it on a normal day, plus about 30% of seaborne LNG. That's nearly a fifth of global oil consumption running through one shipping lane.

<ConceptBox term="Why a chokepoint is different from a supply shock">
A Saudi production cut takes barrels off the market. The market reprices, and
once OPEC+ adjusts, you're back to a new equilibrium. A chokepoint disruption
is different — production hasn't fallen. The barrels exist, sitting in storage
at the wellhead, just unable to get out. The instant the chokepoint clears,
those barrels flood the market. Chokepoint shocks are sharper but also more
reversible.
</ConceptBox>

This is what makes Hormuz traffic the *single highest-information variable* on macro screens right now. Production is fine. Refining is fine. The bottleneck is one waterway, and it has a daily public read.

## Where to actually find the data

The cleanest sources, in order of usefulness:

- The [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/) publishes a weekly oil market report
- Tanker tracking firms (Vortexa, Kpler) publish near-real-time vessel counts; some headline figures hit the wire on Bloomberg and Reuters
- The [EIA](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/) publishes a Today in Energy series that occasionally breaks out chokepoint flow

Through March 25, transit appears to be running at roughly 60% of pre-conflict levels. That's a 40% reduction in the world's most critical oil corridor. The fact Brent is "only" $87 — not $110 — is itself the market's bet that this is short-lived.

## The asymmetry to think about

A useful framing: oil is currently priced for one of two paths.

- **Path A (priced ~70%):** ceasefire by mid-April, Hormuz traffic recovers to 90%+ within four weeks, Brent reverts to $72–75 by Q3. Headline CPI gets a one-month spike then fades.
- **Path B (priced ~30%):** conflict escalates, Hormuz traffic falls below 50%, Brent breaks $100, breakevens move 25 bps higher, the Fed gets cornered into either a hawkish hold the data doesn't support or a credibility-damaging cut.

The probabilities are mine, not the market's; the asymmetry isn't. If you read the curve structure carefully — Brent backwardation has flattened versus February but the long end of the curve has barely moved — the market is firmly in Path A. Which means it's largely the market betting *against* a chokepoint that is literally already shut. That's a strange bet.

## What the rates market is doing

The 10-year sits at 4.42%, up modestly. But the [10-year inflation breakeven](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/T10YIE) is up only 9 bps since the conflict started, to 2.41%. The bond market is quietly endorsing the same Path A read as crude: this passes.

That's a useful disagreement to be aware of. Equities are priced for Path A and freaking out at the volatility. Rates are priced for Path A calmly. Oil itself is priced somewhere between. Either equities are wrong (in which case S&P 500 mean-reverts up 3% on the next ceasefire headline), or rates are wrong (in which case the next CPI print blows out and the curve repriess violently).

I think rates are right and equities are over-reacting. But I am not betting confidently on it.

## What I'm watching next

- **Daily Hormuz transit estimates from Vortexa/Kpler.** If transit drops below 50% of normal in any week, the asymmetry breaks down and Path B starts pricing in fast.
- **Brent backwardation slope.** A flattening front-end vs. unchanged long-end is the market's signature of "spike, then revert." If long-dated futures start moving, conviction in mean-reversion is breaking.
- **April 10 CPI release** ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/schedule/news_release/cpi.htm)). The first read on how much of the oil move passed through into headline. Consensus is +0.5% MoM for headline, +0.30% for core.
- **OPEC+ messaging.** They have spare capacity. If they signal it, that's a bigger price-cap on Brent than any headline-event mean-reversion.

Hormuz is the variable. Oil price is the lagging indicator. If you only have time to watch one number, watch the chokepoint.
