The [March CPI report](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm), released April 10, printed +0.9% month-on-month and +3.3% year-on-year. Both were the highest in a year. If you read the wire copy, you saw "inflation reaccelerates." If you read the report, you saw something different.

Strip out energy, and headline CPI rose just 0.2% in March.

## The single line that explains everything

The energy index rose 10.9% in a single month. Within energy, gasoline rose 21.2% — the largest one-month gasoline move since the Russia-Ukraine spike in 2022. Energy alone accounted for roughly three-quarters of the total monthly CPI increase.

This is exactly what you'd expect from the Iran conflict. Brent went from $70 in late February to $108 at peak. Gasoline lags Brent by about three weeks at the pump. March was the lag period.

<ConceptBox term="Why supercore is the number that matters">
Headline CPI is a weighted average of food, energy, shelter, and core services.
Energy is volatile and supply-driven; the Fed doesn't control oil. Shelter
operates on lease cycles, lagging real rents by 12-18 months. The Fed cares
most about "supercore" — services excluding shelter and energy — because that's
where wages, expectations, and labor-market tightness show up cleanly. It's
the part of inflation the Fed can actually do something about.
</ConceptBox>

## Supercore actually decelerated

Services excluding energy rose 0.2% MoM in March, down from 0.3% in February. The year-on-year supercore measure is now 3.0%, the lowest since June 2024. That is the disinflation the Fed has been waiting for, and it showed up in the same report whose headline screamed reacceleration.

This is going to be the central tension at the May 7 FOMC meeting. The hawks will point to the headline and to upside risks from oil. The doves will point to supercore and call the print a one-month, supply-driven distortion that doesn't change the underlying trend.

The dovish case is supported by the April 8 ceasefire — which means April CPI (released May 12) will likely show energy mean-reverting hard. But the Fed will not have that data on May 7. They will have to decide based on March alone.

## The shelter story is now uninteresting

Shelter rose 0.3% in March, in line with February. Owner's equivalent rent is up 3.4% YoY. Both numbers are continuing their slow grind back toward the 2017-2019 pre-pandemic pace of 3.2%. There is no longer any tension here — shelter is doing what it's supposed to do, and it's doing it on schedule.

This matters because shelter is roughly a third of CPI by weight. With shelter cooperating, the Fed's job is much simpler: it's a question of whether oil stays elevated enough to feed through into services pricing.

## What the bond market did

The 10-year Treasury yield was 4.31% the day of the print, basically flat. The 5-year breakeven moved up 3 bps to 2.46%. That is a calm reaction to a hot headline — confirmation that the bond market believes the energy spike will fade.

The 2-year fell 2 bps to 4.16% on the print. Cuts probability for May moved up slightly, from 22% to 27%, on the supercore softness. Probability for July moved to 71% from 65%. The market is reading the report dovishly *because* it stripped out energy and saw services cooling.

This is one of those situations where the dispersion of how-people-read-the-data is more interesting than the data itself. Retail investors saw a 3.3% headline and panicked. Bond desks saw 0.2% supercore and bid Treasuries.

## What it means for May 7

The base case: the FOMC holds at 3.50–3.75%, ignores the headline distortion, points to supercore deceleration as evidence the disinflation trend is intact, and notes that April data (with oil unwound) will be the cleaner read. Powell will use the word "patient" four or five times.

The committee will not want to send a hawkish signal that requires unwinding two months later when April CPI prints soft. Better to hold quietly.

The interesting decision is what they do with the median dot. The June 17 SEP — the next dot plot — will show whether the March dispersion was transitional or structural. If the median moves to two cuts and the dispersion compresses, the disinflation thesis has firmed. If the median stays at one cut and the dispersion widens further, the committee is genuinely uncertain.

I'd bet on the former — June dots show two cuts, tighter cluster — but it's a 55-45 call.

## What I'm watching next

- **April CPI on May 12.** [BLS calendar](https://www.bls.gov/schedule/news_release/cpi.htm). Should show energy unwinding hard. If headline prints below 0.2% MoM and supercore stays at 0.2%, the cuts conversation reopens loudly.
- **April PCE on May 30.** PCE supercore is the Fed's preferred measure. CPI and PCE diverged in Q1 — keep an eye on whether the gap closes.
- **Q1 ECI on April 30.** Wages from the [Employment Cost Index](https://www.bls.gov/eci/) are the cleanest single read on labor-market tightness. If ECI prints below 0.9% QoQ, supercore disinflation has structural backing.
- **Brent crude.** Currently $94.80. If it sustains a move under $80 by mid-May, the energy contribution to CPI flips from positive to negative, and Q2 looks markedly different.

The headline CPI was hot for a real reason — Iran. The trend underneath is still cooling. Don't let one volatile component tell you a different story than the four it sits next to.
