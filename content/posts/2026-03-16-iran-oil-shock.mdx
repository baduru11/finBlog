Brent crude closed at roughly $82 a barrel on March 13, up from about $70 before tensions in the Persian Gulf escalated in late February. That's a [12% move in twelve trading days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Iran_war_fuel_crisis), driven by what the IEA has called "the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market" — Strait of Hormuz traffic is down sharply, and a meaningful share of the world's seaborne crude moves through that narrow strip.

The interesting thing isn't the oil price. The interesting thing is what hasn't moved with it.

## Three things you'd expect to move

In a textbook oil shock, you'd see four reactions, in roughly this order: oil up, breakevens up, long-end yields up, equities down. Of those four, only the first has clearly happened.

- **Brent**: +12% since late February, now $82
- **5y5y forward breakevens**: +6 bps, to about 2.24% — barely moved
- **10-year Treasury**: 4.36%, essentially flat over the same window
- **S&P 500**: down about 2.1% intraday on the worst day, recovered most of it

That's a strange-looking profile. Either the market thinks the shock is transitory, or it thinks the Fed will lean against it, or both.

## Why breakevens are the interesting tell

<ConceptBox term="Why breakevens matter more than headline oil">
Breakeven inflation rates — the spread between nominal Treasuries and TIPS — are
the cleanest market-implied forecast of inflation. When oil rises but breakevens
don't, the market is saying: "the price is up, but we don't expect it to stay
up, or we don't expect it to feed through." That's a much stronger statement
than the price level alone.
</ConceptBox>

The 5-year breakeven has moved from 2.31% on February 26 to 2.39% on March 13 — only 8 bps. The 5y5y forward, which strips out near-term effects and looks at long-run expected inflation, has moved even less. If the market thought we were genuinely re-anchoring inflation expectations higher, those forwards would be the first place you'd see it.

So far, you don't.

## The Fed problem

The Fed has been clear it will look through "supply-driven" inflation. Energy from the Strait of Hormuz is exactly that. But "look through" is easy when oil is at $82. It gets harder at $100, harder still at $120 — because at some point pass-through into shelter, services, and wages becomes inevitable, and "transitory" becomes the most-mocked word in monetary policy for the second time this decade.

The March 18 [FOMC meeting](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm) is going to be the first read on how the Fed is framing this. I'd expect Powell to say roughly: *headline inflation will tick up, core ex-energy is what we're watching, our job is anchoring expectations.* That's the safe statement. The interesting question is the dots — how many participants raised their 2026 path on the assumption oil stays elevated?

## What the absent moves tell you

You can read the muted reaction in two ways. The optimistic read: the market believes the Strait of Hormuz disruption is short-lived, oil mean-reverts to $70-something by Q3, and breakevens are right not to budge. The pessimistic read: the market is *complacent* — it has been told too many times that geopolitical shocks fade, and is anchored to that prior. We will know which interpretation is right entirely in retrospect.

What I'd bet on: the second read is more plausible than the first if the conflict drags into April. Markets are fast at the first move and slow at the third.

## What I'm watching next

- **March 18 FOMC.** The dots are the message — not the rate level. Watch the dispersion.
- **Strait of Hormuz traffic.** The IEA publishes a weekly read; if April flows recover materially, oil un-spikes. If they don't, $82 is a floor, not a ceiling.
- **Breakevens.** If 5y5y forward breakevens push above 2.50% before the next CPI on April 10, the market is no longer treating this as transitory. That changes the Fed's job.
- **Equity dispersion.** Energy is +9.4% MoM, materials +5.1%, but consumer discretionary -3.8%. If the dispersion widens, the market is starting to price a stagflationary path.

The thing about oil shocks is that they're cleaner in retrospect than in real time. Right now the market is making bets on duration of the disruption. We'll know who was right inside of a quarter.
