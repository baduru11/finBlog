Brent crude fell 13% on April 8, settling at $94.80 a barrel from a session high near $108. WTI fell more than 15% to $95.75. The S&P 500 closed up 2.4%. The dollar weakened. Gold fell 1.7%. The 10-year Treasury yield rose 7 bps to 4.31%.

That is not what an oil-price reset looks like. It is what an unwinding looks like.

## The difference matters

When fundamentals change, prices move at a velocity proportional to how surprising the news is and how much of the move was already priced. A genuine ceasefire is not a surprise — markets had been pricing meaningful probability of one for at least two weeks. Brent went from $87 in mid-March to $103 on April 4, then back to $94.80 on April 8. That round-trip with the ceasefire price closer to where it started than where it peaked is the tell.

What you saw on April 8 was traders who had been long volatility, long energy, and short equities-for-hedge unwinding all three positions in the same session. The triggers were procedural — stops, gamma flips, risk-budget resets — not analytical.

<ConceptBox term="Fundamental versus positioning moves">
A fundamental move is roughly proportional to news content and decays slowly.
A positioning move is much larger than the news warrants, happens in the
first ~120 minutes after the catalyst, and overshoots before settling.
The signature of positioning unwinds: implied volatility collapses (it did,
VIX from 23 to 17 in one session), correlation across assets goes to 1
(everything moved together on April 8), and the cross-asset reaction is
disproportionate to the macro story.
</ConceptBox>

## The numbers that actually matter

If you think about what changed *fundamentally*, only one number is interesting: oil now sits at $94.80, still about $25 above the pre-conflict level near $70. The ceasefire fixed roughly 35-40% of the geopolitical premium. That is meaningful but not transformative — there is still a war-risk premium baked in.

Compare to the breakevens market. The 10-year inflation breakeven barely moved on April 8 — down 4 bps to 2.43%. If anyone genuinely thought oil was going back to $70, breakevens would have collapsed 15 bps. They didn't. The bond market is treating the ceasefire as a reduction in tail risk, not a reset of the central case.

## Why equities ran

The S&P 500 +2.4% looks dramatic, but consider what was priced in. Going into April 8, the index was 4.2% off its March 24 peak. The bounce closed about 60% of that drawdown. In other words: equities priced the ceasefire by erasing roughly the moves they had made *because* of the conflict. From a 30-day window perspective, very little net happened.

The sectors are more interesting:
- **Energy**: -2.8% (gave back about half the conflict-period rally)
- **Airlines & travel**: +5.3% (jet fuel exposure)
- **Defense**: -1.9% (gave back conflict-period gains)
- **Tech**: +2.7% (pure beta, plus relief from rates falling earlier in the day)

This is consistent with the positioning-unwind read. Sector dispersion was modest; the move was broad-based.

## The real question is duration

A ceasefire is fragile. It is, by definition, a pause rather than a peace. Strait of Hormuz traffic recovered to about 78% of pre-conflict levels by April 9 — better than the ~50% trough in late March, but well short of normal. The market is pricing roughly 80% probability the ceasefire holds through Q3.

If it holds, oil mean-reverts toward $80 by July, headline CPI gets a one-month boost from the disruption that's already in the data, and the Fed gets back to its base case. If it doesn't, you re-run the playbook from March 1 — except this time with positioning that's been forced flat.

The consensus view is that ceasefires in this region have a roughly 60% base rate of failure within 90 days. The market is pricing 80% they hold. That's an asymmetry to be aware of, even if you don't act on it.

## What the 10-year yield rose for

This is the move that confused most desks. With oil falling sharply, you'd expect breakevens to fall and the 10-year to fall with them. Instead the yield rose 7 bps, mostly on the long end — a small bear-steepener.

The most likely explanation: the war had been a flight-to-quality bid for Treasuries. The ceasefire removed that bid. Real yields rose alongside breakevens being roughly flat. That's a "risk-on" signature, not an inflation-expectations signature, and it tells you the bond market read the ceasefire as a regime change in risk premium, not in fundamentals.

## What I'm watching next

- **Hormuz transit data through April 22.** If transit recovers above 90% of normal, the residual risk premium in oil drains away and Brent moves toward $80. Below 80%, the premium stays.
- **April 10 CPI** ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm), 8:30 ET). The first quantitative read on how much of the March oil spike fed through into headline. Consensus is +0.5% MoM headline; anything above +0.7% changes the story.
- **VIX through next week.** A sustained move below 15 confirms the positioning-unwind read; a re-spike above 20 says markets don't believe the ceasefire.
- **Brent December 2026 futures.** If the long end of the curve doesn't follow front-month down, the market is pricing structural disruption, not just a ceasefire-led reversion.

The day after a market shock is exactly when the most-confident-sounding narratives are wrong. Treat April 8 as positioning, not fundamentals, and most of the moves get easier to explain.
