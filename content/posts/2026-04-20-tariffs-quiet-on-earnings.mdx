According to FactSet's earnings tracking, [452 S&P 500 companies](https://insight.factset.com/sp-500-earnings-season-update-april-17-2026) mentioned "tariff" or "tariffs" on their Q1 2025 earnings calls. That made trade policy the defining narrative of last year's reporting season.

Through the first two weeks of Q1 2026 reporting, the count is dramatically lower. Geopolitical risk — specifically the US-Iran conflict — has taken the top spot. Tariffs are now a secondary concern, mentioned mostly in passing.

This is being read by the sell side as good news. I think it is the wrong read.

## What changed and what didn't

What changed: the *rhetorical* environment. Tariffs that were proposed in early 2025 have largely been implemented. The shock is in the run rate. CEOs aren't warning about a coming cliff because the cliff already happened.

What didn't change: the *effective tariff rate*. According to Charles Schwab's tracking, the average effective US import tariff is still in double digits — among the highest the modern US economy has lived under. That cost is now permanent until Congress or the executive branch acts. It hasn't been priced out; it has just been absorbed.

When CEOs stop mentioning a headwind on calls, two things can be true. They've stopped expecting it to get worse (good). Or they've baked it into guidance and stopped flagging it (also fine). What is rarely true is that the headwind itself has gone away.

<ConceptBox term="Why mention-counts on earnings calls are noisy">
A mention count is a measure of communication, not of impact. Companies stop
talking about an issue when (a) it stops being uncertain — meaning it's
already in the numbers — or (b) it stops being newsworthy. Neither requires
the issue to actually have resolved. The signal in mention-count changes is
about *where attention is*, not about what's affecting fundamentals.
</ConceptBox>

## The earnings numbers tell a different story

Q1 earnings, with about 75% of companies reported, are tracking +13.2% year-on-year — the sixth consecutive quarter of double-digit growth. That sounds great. But:

- Tech earnings: +45.0% YoY (and 90%+ of the index's earnings beat)
- Materials: +24.2% YoY
- Financials: +15.1% YoY
- Consumer Discretionary: -2.1% YoY
- Energy: +5.8% YoY (less than expected — tariffs on equipment imports have hit operators)

The headline +13% is being driven almost entirely by AI capex spending and a few mega-cap tech earnings beats. Strip out the top five companies (Microsoft, Nvidia, Apple, Meta, Amazon) and S&P 500 earnings growth is closer to +4-5% — not bad, but not consensus-beating either.

That bifurcation has been a story for two years. What's new in Q1 is that the bottom-half of the index is starting to show tariff-related cost pressures in margins, even as the top mostly avoids them through scale and supply-chain optionality.

## Where to look for the tariff effects

Three categories of companies will tell you whether tariffs are working through earnings the way they should:

1. **Discount retailers** (Dollar Tree, Five Below). Their cost basis is heavily import-dependent and their pricing power is limited. Margins should compress.
2. **Auto OEMs and parts** (Ford, Aptiv). Steel and aluminum costs feed directly into their COGS.
3. **Apparel** (Hanes, VF, Levi's). Roughly 95% of US apparel is imported. Tariff pass-through goes to consumers, but only with a lag.

Q1 reports for category 1 are showing 60-100 bps of gross margin compression that wasn't in guidance three months ago. Category 2 is showing a rotation toward Mexican-sourced parts (USMCA-exempt) at the expense of Asian. Category 3 is starting to raise prices, ~3% across SKUs, with the inflation showing up in May-June CPI.

None of this is in the "tariffs are no longer a story" narrative. All of it is.

## Why the market doesn't care yet

Three reasons:
1. **AI is dominating the index.** As long as the top 5 keep beating, broad-index earnings keep growing.
2. **The Iran conflict is louder.** A geopolitical shock that takes oil from $70 to $108 makes structural tariff drag look small in any given week.
3. **Fed expectations are dovish.** Fed funds futures price one to two cuts by year-end. If the Fed eases into a tariff-driven cost-of-goods inflation, that is medicine, not poison.

But the AI spend is concentrated in a small number of names. Iran will fade (the April 8 ceasefire is still holding). And the Fed's dovish path requires supercore inflation to keep cooling, which the tariff drag is starting to slow.

The market has decided tariffs are no longer a story. The earnings underneath the index suggest tariffs are very much still a story — they just got drowned out.

## What I'd watch

- **Earnings revisions in cyclicals.** If forward EPS estimates for industrials and consumer discretionary keep getting trimmed at the current pace, the bottom-half of the index is in earnings recession by Q3.
- **Goods CPI excluding gasoline.** This was 0.0% YoY in March. If it turns positive in April-June reads, tariff pass-through has begun in earnest.
- **The ISM Manufacturing PMI prices-paid index.** Currently at 58 — elevated. A move above 65 would be a clear tariff-pass-through signature.
- **The May 7 FOMC.** Will [the FOMC](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm) explicitly mention tariff-driven cost pressures in the statement? If yes, that's a signal they're worried about persistence. If they don't, they've decided to absorb it.

The story isn't that tariffs have gone away. The story is that everyone except the companies actually paying them stopped talking about them. Those are different things.
