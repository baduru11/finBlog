The Federal Reserve held the policy rate at 3.50–3.75% on April 29. The next morning, the BEA published [Q1 2026 GDP advance estimate](https://www.bea.gov/news/2026/gdp-advance-estimate-1st-quarter-2026) at 2.0% annualised — below the 2.3% consensus. The 10-year Treasury yield rose 6 bps to 4.41% on the FOMC, then drifted lower into the GDP print, settling at 4.39% on May 1.

These two events sit awkwardly in the wire-copy narrative: the Fed held rates because the economy is firm, and then the GDP print said the economy is softer than expected. That makes the hold look reactive and almost wrong.

It wasn't. Read in sequence, the Fed and the BEA are reading the same data the same way.

## What the GDP print actually showed

Real GDP rose 2.0% annualised in Q1. That's a sharp acceleration from Q4 2025's +0.5%. The composition:

- **Personal consumption**: +1.4 percentage point contribution (consumer spending growing ~2.3% annualised)
- **Business investment**: +0.6 pp (driven heavily by AI capex)
- **Government**: +0.3 pp
- **Net exports**: -0.4 pp (tariff drag on imports rebound)
- **Inventories**: +0.1 pp

The miss versus consensus was almost entirely in net exports — imports rebounded faster than expected, partly as companies rebuilt inventory ahead of further tariff escalation. Strip out net exports and final domestic demand grew 2.4% — actually stronger than the headline.

<ConceptBox term="Final domestic demand">
GDP includes net exports, which are noisy quarter-to-quarter — they reflect
trade timing as much as demand. Final domestic demand (consumption + investment
+ government, excluding inventories and trade) is the cleaner measure of how
hot the underlying economy is running. If headline GDP misses but final demand
beats, the miss is about trade timing, not weakness.
</ConceptBox>

That distinction is exactly what the Fed was looking at when it held the day before.

## The April FOMC: hold, but with three subtle changes

Powell's statement and presser on April 29 contained three small but meaningful tone shifts versus March:

1. **Removed "data-dependent" framing for cuts.** The March statement said the Committee would proceed cautiously with respect to any policy adjustments. April's was tighter: "we are not in a hurry to adjust our policy stance." Subtle shift from "we'll see" to "we don't need to."

2. **Acknowledged the energy spike was transitory.** Powell explicitly cited the April 8 ceasefire and "rapid normalization in energy prices" as reasons the March CPI overshoot would not persist. That's the cleanest signal yet that supercore (still 3.0% YoY) is what they're targeting.

3. **Mentioned "balance of risks shifting toward employment."** Three months ago, the balance language emphasized inflation. Today it doesn't. That is a small but unmistakable dovish tilt.

The 10-year jumped 6 bps to 4.416% on the day. Why? Two reasons. The market read "no hurry to adjust" hawkishly — a few cuts had been priced in for May/June. And the 30-year auction earlier in the day tailed by 1.5 bps, suggesting weak duration demand. Both pulled rates higher.

But the curve flattened bull-steepener style: the 2-year was nearly flat, the long end rose. That is the market telling you it didn't move its policy expectation; it moved its term-premium expectation.

## Why the two decisions agree

Imagine you saw the same data the FOMC saw — through April 28, before the GDP print. You would have seen:
- March CPI hot but heavily energy-driven
- March supercore decelerating to +0.2% MoM
- March payrolls strong (178k) but quality narrow
- April 8 ceasefire reducing oil-price tail risk
- Q1 GDP not yet reported, with [Atlanta Fed GDPNow](https://www.atlantafed.org/cqer/research/gdpnow) tracking 2.0%

You would have concluded: economy still firm, inflation cooling underneath the headline noise, no urgent need to ease but also no urgent reason to tighten. That is exactly what the FOMC concluded, and exactly what the GDP advance estimate confirmed the next morning.

The wire copy treats the two events as unrelated; they are not. The Fed has access to high-frequency private data and to the GDPNow estimate. The 2.0% print on April 30 was within 30 basis points of what the Fed was modeling on April 28. The hold was not "lucky"; it was a calibrated decision based on a near-final picture of Q1 demand that the BEA later confirmed.

## What the equity market read

S&P 500 closed at a new all-time high of 7,230.12 on May 1, +0.29%. Apple led, partly on a stronger-than-expected services-revenue growth read in its earnings the night before. Nvidia and Microsoft both pushed to fresh highs.

That tape is consistent with: tariffs absorbed, Iran ceasefire holding, Fed holding, AI capex resilient. None of that is contradicted by 2.0% GDP. Equities cared more about earnings (+13% YoY blended) than about a 30-bp GDP miss that was driven by trade timing.

## What the bond market read

The 2-year is at 4.18%, basically flat post-FOMC. Fed funds futures imply 18% probability of a May 7 cut (down from 27% before the meeting), 65% by July, 90% by September. The path of cuts hasn't shifted much — the *timing* shifted slightly later.

The 10-year at 4.39% is roughly back to where it was in early April. The 2s10s curve is +21 bps, lightly positive — same shape as my late April analysis. Term premium is doing the work in the long end.

This is a coherent picture: cuts later, fewer, with term premium absorbing the tariff and supply-side noise.

## What I'm watching next

- **April CPI on May 12.** [BLS](https://www.bls.gov/schedule/news_release/cpi.htm). Energy should unwind hard; if supercore prints +0.2% or below and headline +0.2% or below, the cuts conversation gets louder fast.
- **Q1 GDP second estimate May 28.** Where the trade and inventory numbers get firmer. Watch the consumption revision — that's the variable that matters most for Q2.
- **April nonfarm payrolls May 2.** Whether the participation-rate slip in March was noise or signal.
- **Fed-speak between now and May 7 next year is the wrong meeting** — there is no May 7 meeting next, the next is June 17 with a fresh SEP. Watch dispersion in dot-related comments from regional bank presidents in the meantime.

The Fed didn't get lucky on April 29. It made the same read the data confirmed the next morning. That is what good monetary policy looks like — boring and right.
