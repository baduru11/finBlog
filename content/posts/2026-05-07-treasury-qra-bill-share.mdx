Two Treasury announcements landed this week. On May 4, the borrowing estimate for the April–June quarter was raised to [\$189 billion](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0485), \$79 billion higher than the February projection. On May 6, the [Quarterly Refunding Statement](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0489) kept nominal coupon and FRN auction sizes unchanged for "at least the next several quarters." The 10-year yield drifted from 4.44% on May 5 down to 4.37% by Wednesday's close.

Read together, the two announcements look like they cancel each other out: more borrowing, same auctions, and a yield that fell anyway. They don't cancel. The bill share is doing the work.

## Three facts to anchor on

**One.** Q2 marketable borrowing is now estimated at \$189 billion, with an end-of-June cash balance assumption of \$900 billion. Q3 is projected at \$671 billion, with an end-September cash assumption of \$950 billion. Q1 actual borrowing was \$577 billion. The Q2-to-Q3 step-up — from \$189 billion to \$671 billion — is the largest sequential jump in the post-debt-ceiling cycle.

**Two.** The May refunding will sell \$58 billion of 3-year notes on May 11, \$42 billion of 10-year notes on May 12, and \$25 billion of 30-year bonds on May 13. Together, the \$125 billion in coupon issuance refunds \$83.3 billion of maturing securities and raises \$41.7 billion in new cash. None of those numbers moved from the February refunding. Treasury also kept buyback capacity at up to \$38 billion in off-the-run liquidity-support operations and up to \$25 billion in cash-management buybacks across the next quarter.

**Three.** The bill share of marketable debt is on track to rise from roughly 21.6% to 23.2% over the year, with 4-week bills now averaging \$101 billion per auction — the largest single Treasury security on the calendar. <ConceptBox term="TBAC">The Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee has, since 2024, framed a 20% bill share as the upper end of "appropriate." Anything above is described as temporary.</ConceptBox> The current trajectory has now spent most of two years above the band, with no projected return inside it.

## Why "no change" is the change

If you only watch coupon auction sizes, the Treasury looks boring. Auctions held flat through the regional-bank stress of 2023, through the deficit revision shock of August 2024, through the Iran oil spike in March 2026, and through this week. Secretary Bessent's QRA kept the "at least" language that signals no near-term increase. Dealers had positioned for that, and the rally on May 6 — 10s down 6 bps to 4.37% — was the relief trade.

The story underneath is fiscal supply migrating to the front end. The Q2 estimate moved up \$79 billion, but every coupon auction stayed the same. The arithmetic only closes if bills absorb the difference. They are: 4-week and 8-week sizes have ratcheted higher through April, and the TBAC minutes from May 5 explicitly note that Treasury will "further increase offering sizes of shorter-dated benchmark bills over the coming weeks."

<Callout variant="insight" label="Takeaway">
A flat coupon calendar plus a higher borrowing estimate is not a contradiction. It is a decision to keep the long end quiet and route the marginal funding through bills.
</Callout>

## What it costs, and who pays for it

Bills are the cheapest place to fund when the curve is upward-sloping and money-market demand is deep. Both conditions held in May 2026: front-end bill yields sit well inside coupon yields out to 30 years, and money-market fund AUM has continued to grow through Q1 2026. The cost is rollover speed: every dollar that funds the deficit through 4-week bills reprices roughly thirteen times a year. <ConceptBox term="WAM">Weighted-average maturity of US marketable debt has been drifting lower since 2023; rising bill share extends that trend.</ConceptBox>

The implication for the long end is the more interesting part. If Treasury is willing to ration duration by holding coupon sizes flat in the face of an upward-revised borrowing path, then duration term premium should be lower at the margin than the borrowing path alone would imply. The 10-year yield easing 6 bps on the announcement day, despite a \$79 billion upward revision two days earlier, is consistent with that: the long end is being managed by issuance choice as much as by data.

The risk is the inverse. If money-market demand softens — most plausibly through a Fed cut that compresses bill yields and pulls cash into prime funds and short coupons — bills become a less convenient sink, and Treasury would have to start raising coupon sizes. That is the reaction function dealers will price first.

## What I'm watching

- **May 8 employment situation report.** April ADP printed +109,000 against a 84,000 consensus; the BLS print at 8:30 a.m. ET will set the pre-auction tone. A soft headline pulls front-end yields lower and makes bills easier to issue. A hot headline does the opposite.
- **May 13 30-year auction.** First test of long-end demand into the upward-revised borrowing path. Watch the bid-to-cover and the indirect bidder share against the six-month average; a meaningful tail would be a signal that flat coupon sizes are not free.
- **June 16 settlement-timing change.** From this date, [20-year reopenings settle on the Friday of auction week](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0489) rather than at month end. Plumbing changes like this one are a tell that Treasury is engineering more flexibility into the schedule before it has to announce a coupon increase.
- **August 2026 refunding.** If Q3 borrowing tracks toward \$671 billion and bill share rises through 24%, the August QRA is where the "at least" guidance will either be reaffirmed or quietly retired.
- **Money-market fund flows.** Weekly ICI data through May 1 still showed net inflows. Sustained outflows of more than \$50 billion a week would be the first crack in the bill-absorption story.
