---
term: Bill share
shortDef: The fraction of total marketable Treasury debt issued as bills (maturity of one year or less) rather than as coupon-bearing notes and bonds.
category: rates
---

The Treasury Department finances the federal deficit by issuing a mix of short-dated bills (maturities of 4, 8, 13, 17, 26, and 52 weeks) and longer-dated coupon-bearing notes and bonds (2 to 30 years). The bill share is simply the portion of outstanding marketable debt held in bills.

Bills are cheaper to issue when the curve is upward-sloping and easier to absorb because money-market funds and bank cash desks need them as collateral. The trade-off is rollover risk: bills get reissued every few weeks, so a heavier bill share means deficit financing reprices faster when rates move.

The Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee (TBAC) has historically suggested an "appropriate" long-run bill share around 15–20%. When the share runs above 20%, TBAC frames it as a temporary tool, not a target. In practice, the share has spent most of the post-2023 period above that band — the bill share is doing the work that auction sizes don't show.
